An Ordered Probit PMT Model: targeting the poor with synthetic Susenas 2022–2025

Fit a proxy means test as an ordered probit on the welfare decile, next to its linear counterpart; learn it on three survey rounds, predict a fourth round the model has never seen, and measure how well each prediction finds the poorest half of the population. Four do files are given to you partly written. Finish them so that they run from start to end and print the reference output exactly.

1. The task

A proxy means test (PMT) predicts a household's welfare from things a programme officer can see or ask at the door: the house, its water and sanitation, the assets, who lives there. The welfare measure itself, per-capita consumption expenditure, is only known from a survey. The PMT is fitted on survey data and then applied to households outside the survey.

In this assignment the survey is Susenas March, four rounds. You will:

  • read the four rounds as BPS delivers them (dBase files, several files per round) and build one household-level analysis file per round;
  • fit two linear PMT models on the pooled rounds 2022–2024 with district fixed effects: the full BPS/World Bank specification of thirty-nine proxies, and a parsimonious specification of eight underlying variables;
  • fit the same two specifications as ordered probit models on the welfare decile;
  • use each of the four models to predict the 2025 round, select the predicted bottom half of the population, and compute the inclusion and exclusion errors of that rule.

The data are synthetic. They were generated from the real Susenas so that the structure, the file layout and the results are realistic, but no record belongs to a real household. Treat them as you would treat real microdata; the skills are the same.

4. Building the analysis file

Each round becomes one file, analysis_YYYY.dta, with one row per household. The steps, in the order the given 2022 block does them:

4.1 Person counts

From the individual file, build 0/1 indicators per person and collapse (sum) them by household. The head of the household is the person with relationship code 1; the head's age and certificate are taken with (max) after keeping them only on the head's row. Two questions asked of every person, phone ownership and internet use, are carried as household characteristics by taking the first listed member. Count the members with (count); this count is checked later against the household file.

4.2 BLOK42 from long to wide

BLOK42 has one row per household per item. Keep the three items, put the quantity of electricity and the values of phone credit and internet packages into three variables, and collapse (max) by household. Households that bought none of the three items still have one all-zero row in the module; keep that row, so that these households come out with zeros rather than disappear.

Item codes In the March 2022 file the three items are code 205 (electricity, quantity in kWh in b42k3), 236 (phone credit, value in b42k4) and 238 (internet packages, value in b42k4). Check the codes of every other round in its metadata workbook or by tabulating kode.

4.3 Merge

Start from the household file, rename the housing and asset fields to the common names of section 5, then merge one-to-one on urut: BLOK43 (keep food nonfood expend kapita wert weind), the person counts, the BLOK42 variables. Then merge the poverty line many-to-one on year, province and urban/rural. Every merge must match every household; the given code keeps matched rows only and the checks below catch a failed merge.

4.4 Check before trusting

Household size can be got three ways: round(expend / kapita), the household file's r301, and the number of person rows. All three agree in these data. The given code asserts this, and that food + nonfood = expend and weind = wert × hhsize. If an assert fails in a round you wrote, the error is in that round's merge or collapse.

4.5 The welfare measure

rpcexp = kapita × 738 955 / povline    lnrpcexp = ln(rpcexp)

Monthly per-capita expenditure is deflated by the poverty line of the household's year, province and urban/rural cell, and re-expressed in the prices of one cell: DKI Jakarta urban, March 2022, whose poverty line is Rp 738 955. One decile ranking then means the same living standard in every province and every year.

4.6 Deciles

Two rules. The training rounds 2022, 2023 and 2024 are appended and ranked together into ten weighted deciles of rpcexp, so that decile 1 means the same in each year. The 2025 round is ranked on its own: it is the test round, and nothing from it may enter the training ranking. Deciles are weighted with weind, the individual weight, so that a decile is a tenth of people, not of households. weind is rounded to an integer first because xtile takes frequency weights.

5.5 Structural missing values

Four variables are blank for a reason, not at random: h_closet and h_sewage when the household has no toilet, h_epower when lighting is not PLN metered, and hd_edu for heads with no certificate recorded. A factor variable with a blank drops the household from the regression without a message. Step 2 recodes all four to the explicit category 99 and prints how many households each recode keeps; those counts are part of the reference output.

All four models are fitted on the pooled training rounds 2022–2024 (train == 1, 1 024 865 households) and predict every household, including the 343 460 households of the 2025 test round. Every model carries an urban dummy and a fixed effect for each of the 514 districts (i.kode_kab); the district effects are part of the model but are not printed.

Model 3 — ordered probit on the decile, the PMT, district fixed effects

decileh = k  if  κk−1 < αd(h) + Xhβ + γ·urbanh + uh ≤ κk,   u ~ N(0, 1),  k = 1 … 10

The dependent variable is the training decile of section 4.6. One slope vector and nine cutpoints κ1 … κ9 are estimated; the cutpoints appear at the foot of the coefficient table. From the fitted model take the ten predicted probabilities per household and their probability-weighted mean, the expected decile:

op_expectedh = Σk=1..10 k · Pr(decileh = k | Xh)

generate double op_expected = 1*op1 + 2*op2 + ... + 10*op10

Model 4 — ordered probit on the decile, the parsimonious model, district fixed effects

Same estimator with $predictors_7. Probabilities ops1 … ops10, expected decile ops_expected.

Runtime Each ordered probit carries the proxies and 514 district dummies and is fitted by maximum likelihood. It converges in four or five iterations, about three minutes on a four-core machine. Do not interrupt it.

7. Targeting measures

A programme that targets the poorest half applies the same rule to each model: rank the 2025 households by the model's prediction, weighted by weind, cut the ranking into ten deciles, and select predicted deciles 1–5. The truly eligible are the households in real deciles 1–5, the decile variable of section 4.6 ranked within 2025.

IE50 = people selected who are not eligiblepeople selected       EE50 = eligible people who are not selectedeligible people

Both are weighted shares of people, in per cent. The inclusion error is what a programme pays for people who should not be in; the exclusion error is what the poor lose when they are missed. Because a ranking rule selects exactly as many people as are eligible, the number wrongly selected equals the number wrongly missed, and the two errors are equal (up to the rounding of the decile cut).

For the ordered probit models the score that is ranked is the expected decile. The alternative, taking the most likely decile, cannot fill a half-population quota and is not used here.

xtile d_pmt = yhat_pmt [fweight = weind] if train == 0, nquantiles(10)
quietly summarize weind if train == 0 & d_pmt <= 5, meanonly
scalar selected_pmt = r(sum)
quietly summarize weind if train == 0 & decile <= 5, meanonly
scalar eligible_pmt = r(sum)
quietly summarize weind if train == 0 & d_pmt <= 5 & decile > 5, meanonly
scalar ie_pmt = 100 * r(sum) / selected_pmt
quietly summarize weind if train == 0 & d_pmt > 5 & decile <= 5, meanonly
scalar ee_pmt = 100 * r(sum) / eligible_pmt

The same block, with the other three scores, gives the four rows of the final table.

9. Rules, runtime and submission

  • Write every round and every model out in full, as the given blocks are. Do not use a loop over rounds or models, and do not write a program: the reader of your file, including you in six months, should see which variable belongs to which round.
  • Use forward slashes in every path. Stata accepts them on Windows, and a backslash immediately before a macro name is an escape character that fails without a message.
  • Keep the given variable names, file names and globals. Steps read one another's files.
  • Run the whole chain with do s_run_all.do after editing its three path lines. Runtime is about fifteen minutes on a four-core machine: two for the import, four for the build, four for the linear models, six for the ordered probits.
  • Hand in the four completed do files and student.log, the log that s_run_all.do writes.

Two questions to answer in a short note with your submission: model 2 has a higher R² than model 1 — why might a programme still prefer model 1? And modelling the decile directly (models 3 and 4) does not target better than modelling expenditure and cutting it afterwards — what information does the ordered model throw away before it starts?
